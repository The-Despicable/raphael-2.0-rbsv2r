Abstract
Penetration testing is an effective means of ensuring system secu-
rity, yet it suffers from drawbacks such as being time-consuming,
labor-intensive, and requiring highly specialized operators. The
advancement of large language models (LLMs) has made it feasible
to utilize them for automated penetration testing. In this paper, we
propose AutoPen, a method designed to autonomously conduct
penetration testing without human intervention. AutoPen employs
techniques such as Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), chain-
of-thought reasoning, and structured output to obtain more relevant
and accurate responses from the LLM. Furthermore, it incorporates
a carefully designed penetration testing workflow with task decom-
position and specialized agents to ensure efficient and autonomous
operation. We conducted penetration testing experiments on vari-
ous vulnerable hosts from VulnHub. The results demonstrate that
AutoPen performs automated penetration testing with high sta-
bility and exhibits significantly superior capability compared to
open-source methods and other approaches described in related
literature.

1 Introduction
Penetration testing, sometimes called penetration testing or ethi-
cal hacking[1], is a critical technique for countering cyber threats,
aimed at preventing attacks by identifying security vulnerabilities
within a system. Traditionally, penetration testing is performed
manually by skilled security experts. The conventional process
involves scanning target systems, synthesizing information to iden-
tify entry points, testing for vulnerabilities, and reporting the final
results[2]. While penetration testing significantly enhances system
security, the manual execution of this process demands substantial
labor, time, and extensive expertise and practical experience from
the operator.
Researchers have never ceased their exploration of fully auto-
mated penetration testing that operates without human interven-
tion. Early researchers assumed that defenders possessed complete
observability and modeled attack planning as an attack graph prob-
lem. However, such methods lacked the flexible adaptability re-
quired in real-world environments. Subsequent studies introduced
uncertainty, modeling attack planning as a Markov Decision Process
(MDP) and employing various reinforcement learning algorithms to
identify optimal attack paths[4]. For instance, Yousefi et al. utilized
the MulVaL tool to generate attack graphs and transition matrices,
which were combined with the Q-learning algorithm to produce
attack paths with maximum reward values[5]. As an extension of
the MDP approach, researchers adopted the Partially Observable
Markov Decision Process (POMDP)[6] and reinforcement learning
algorithms[7, 8] to further address uncertainties in the environment
and action outcomes. These methods better reflect the realistic sce-
nario where attackers have limited knowledge of the target system.
Nevertheless, most of these studies remain largely theoretical and
lack the capability for practical deployment and application.
Recent significant advancements in Large Language Models
(LLMs) have led to rapid improvements in their generative capabil-
ities. General-purpose LLMs such as ChatGPT[9] and Deepseek[10,
11], are demonstrating robust performance and introducing new
transformations and opportunities across various fields. This progress
also makes automated penetration testing utilizing LLMs a possi-
bility. To address the inherent limitations of general-purpose LLMs,
existing methods employ multi-agent designs based on role-playing,

dividing the entire penetration testing process into a series of sub-
tasks handled by specialized agents. By standardizing prompt out-
puts, these approaches guide LLMs to generate precisely required
content and avoid overly long dialogues, thereby making effec-
tive use of historical information[12]. Building on this foundation,
subsequent research has introduced the first open benchmark for
LLM-based automated penetration testing [13] and proposed using
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) to provide external knowl-
edge for fine-tuning LLMs, thereby enhancing their domain-specific
expertise [14]. Nevertheless, these methods still cannot eliminate
the reliance on human expertise, requiring manual intervention
for strategic decision-making and specific implementation. Conse-
quently, the proficiency of the operator significantly influences the
testing progression and final outcomes.
Therefore, we propose AutoPen, an attempt to construct a method
capable of autonomously executing penetration testing without
human intervention. In this work, we design an LLM-enhanced pen-
etration testing framework based on a multi-agent architecture and
introduce a novel partitioning of the penetration testing workflow
to ensure its autonomous operation. Functions as orchestrating
the process, logging activities, handling error correction and roll-
back are realized by LLM agents ,which helps process operation.
By organizing information into a knowledge graph, we facilitate
the automated penetration testing process, while critical issues
are addressed through multi-agent integrated responses to miti-
gate hallucinations. Finally, we implement the proposed method
and construct experiments to validate its effectiveness. The main
contributions of this paper are as follows.
• To address the issue of low automation levels and heavy
reliance on human expertise in existing penetration test-
ing methods, we partition the penetration testing workflow
into distinct stages, each managed by a specialized agent.
Furthermore, we meticulously design Inspection Agent to
orchestrate the process and ensure robust operation. Informa-
tion is organized into a knowledge graph for efficient access,
guaranteeing that the penetration testing can be completed
autonomously without external human intervention.
• To overcome the limitations of general-purpose LLMs—such
as limited penetration testing knowledge, susceptibility to
infinite loops due to ambiguous instructions or formats, and
tendencies towards hallucination, which hinder their ability
to successfully complete tests—we employ techniques in-
cluding Retrieval-Augmented Generation , chain-of-thought
reasoning, and structured output to enhance the LLMs’ gen-
erative performance. We also propose a multi-agent inte-
gration approach to mitigate generation errors, ensuring
in-depth testing of the target host can be conducted.
• We implemented the proposed method and constructed a spe-
cific experimental environment to validate its effectiveness.
The results demonstrate that AutoPen can effectively com-
plete penetration testing tasks with high stability, exhibiting
superior performance compared to similar methods.
2 METHODOLOGY
2.1 System Overview
Given the limited context window of large language models, pro-
longed operation would lead to the loss of early-stage information,
making it infeasible to complete the entire penetration testing pro-
cess within a single session. Adopting a role-based agent design
approach, we established three distinct modules within the large
model framework: a Scanning Agent (SA) for environment recon-
naissance, an Analysis Agent (AA) for vulnerability analysis, and
an Execution Agent (EA) for vulnerability exploitation. By decom-
posing the penetration testing workflow into a series of subtasks
assigned to these specialized agents, we significantly reduce the
context length required for each agent’s large model. This approach
also enhances the model’s focus and performance by assigning
more specific and well-defined tasks. Furthermore, given the high
interdependence between different stages of penetration testing,
it is crucial to summarize the information and conclusions gen-
erated by the large model and pass them to subsequent tasks. To
address this, we designed an Inspection Agent (IA) responsible for
self-inspection and backtracking, which automates this information
handover. This design ensures minimal loss of critical information
while maintaining a clear focus at each stage.
Unlike the other agents, the IA is not responsible for generating
or executing specific commands. Instead, it serves three primary
functions: orchestration, self-inspection, and backtracking. Orches-
tration involves managing the automated execution of the overall
workflow from a macro perspective, accurately transmitting infor-
mation between different modules, and generating the final pen-
etration test report by retrieving historical logs upon completion.
Self-inspection refers to promptly detecting errors in other mod-
ules, halting their execution, and attempting to diagnose and rectify
the issue based on the context and error messages. Backtracking
is initiated when the process cannot proceed; the agent then rolls
back operations to a previous stable state and attempts to explore
alternative paths.
The specific operational workflow of these agents is illustrated
in Fig. 1. The functions of each agent and the role of the IA within
each stage will be detailed in Sections 2.B to 2.D, corresponding to
the different phases of the penetration test.
2.2 Environmental Detection
The primary objective of the initial phase of penetration testing
is to comprehensively gather information about the target system,
such as all open ports, running services, and their versions. In this
phase, the IA specifies the target IP address to the SA. The SA then
enters a self-iterative loop, autonomously generating and executing
reconnaissance commands. The results of each command execution
are returned to the SA, which analyzes the outcomes, summarizes
the information, and subsequently generates and executes the next
scanning step until the process is complete. Upon completion of the
scan, the IA receives a results report—including open ports, running
services, and their versions—along with a log file documenting the
entire scanning process, both of which are utilized in subsequent
phases.

this study meticulously designs textual task instructions provided

2.3 Vulnerability Analysis
Upon receiving the target system information provided by the IA,
the AA is responsible for performing vulnerability analysis. To ad-
dress the limitation of insufficient penetration testing knowledge,
we employ Retrieval-Augmented Generation technology, construct-
ing a knowledge base by extracting key information from relevant
sources to enhance the large language model’s performance. Since
direct online searches often yield redundant and irrelevant infor-
mation, resulting in low efficiency, we utilize the RAG technique to
extract crucial details from the raw search results. RAG combines in-
formation retrieval with large language models by fetching relevant
information from an external knowledge base and using it as con-
textual prompts for the model, thereby enhancing its reasoning and
generative capabilities for complex, domain-specific problems. The
AA compiles and summarizes the search results, retrieves relevant
segments as instructed (such as applicable versions and execution
prerequisites), extracts key information, and ultimately constructs
a concise vulnerability exploitation database.
During runtime, the Vulnerability Analysis Agent performs two
rounds of online searches based on the services and applications
running on the open ports of the target system to gather informa-
tion on potential vulnerabilities. The objective of the first search
is to query search engines and public vulnerability databases to
identify potential attack surfaces of the target system. The second
search leverages the potential attack surfaces identified in the first
round to further search for open-source vulnerability exploitation
code, tutorials, and other relevant resources, thereby obtaining
program-level attack knowledge. These retrieved documents are
stored locally for reference in subsequent penetration testing stages.
Considering that standard Retrieval-Augmented Generation strug-
gles to capture complex semantic relationships within documents,
we pre-process the documents by organizing them into a Knowl-
edge Graph (KG) to facilitate efficient retrieval and reasoning by the
large language model. After the vulnerability exploitation database
is constructed, we further utilize it to build a Knowledge Graph. The
knowledge graph is formally defined as a directed graph G=(V,E),
where: V represents the set of nodes, which includes categories
such as system nodes, software nodes, vulnerability nodes, and
resource nodes; E represents the set of directed edges, describing
relationships between nodes, e.g., “runs”, “has_vuln”, and “has_res”.

2.4 Vulnerability Exploit
On obtaining the knowledge graph, the IA initiates the process by
analyzing the potential attack surfaces. It references service/application
versions and vulnerability types to formulate a penetration testing
plan aimed at gaining and escalating access to the target system.
This plan comprises a series of exploitable vulnerabilities, sorted by
confidence level, along with references to their associated locations
of documentation. The IA then sequentially invokes the EA to test
each vulnerability. The role of the EA is to generate and execute a
set of penetration testing actions constituting a single attack plan,
with the objective of compromising the target system. Agents first
utilizes RAG to access local documentation, thereby enhancing
its expertise and performance. It then generates detailed steps for
vulnerability exploitation and automatically executes them until
success is achieved or the plan is completed.
After all individual attack plans are executed, the IA synthesizes
all the returned results into a comprehensive document. This docu-
ment is provided to the EA with instructions for a final penetration
attempt. During this attempt, the EA is required to exercise creative,
expert-like judgment based on the provided document, striving to
uncover security vulnerabilities in the target system as thoroughly
and deeply as possible. The outcome of this final attempt serves as
the primary basis for the ultimate penetration test report.
When the IA generates an attack plan, the lack of complete infor-
mation necessitates that LLMs supplement certain details to finalize
the plan’s generation. To mitigate potential hallucinations intro-
duced by the LLM during this process, we designed a Multi-Agent
Integration approach. This involves initiating multiple agents to
generate responses for complex tasks, followed by using the LLM
itself to review and eliminate errors resulting. In the described work-
flow, operational errors generated by the specific task agents—SA,
AA, and EA—are corrected by the IA. However, errors originat-
ing from the IA itself pose a significant challenge: designing an

additional dedicated error-correction module could itself malfunc-
tion, potentially leading to an infinite loop of error correction. To
minimize the possibility of the IA producing erroneous results on
critical issues, we integrated the Multi-Agent approach directly into
its reflection process to enhance reliability. Specifically, we initiate
a separate LLM session dedicated to “prompt variation. " This ses-
sion mutates the original input prompt into multiple versions that
are semantically equivalent but phrased differently. These varied
prompts are then dispatched simultaneously to multiple indepen-
dent sessions. The final response is derived by synthesizing the
answers from all these sessions. This method of generating and
consolidating diverse responses helps to average out inconsisten-
cies and mitigate the impact of hallucinations from any single LLM
instance.
The Execution Agent exhibits a high error rate when attempting
to exploit a single vulnerability outlined in the attack plan. This is
partly due to its susceptibility to entering infinite Q&A loops caused
by formatting issues or interpretational discrepancies, and partly
because an error in any single step can lead to the complete failure
of the whole vulnerability exploitation attempt. To address this, we
enhanced the IA with an inspection and backtracking capability.
This functionality allows the IA to review the failure logs returned
by the EA. If the review confirms the step was executed correctly but
simply failed (e.g., the vulnerability was not present or exploitable),
the IA proceeds with the overall attack plan. However, if the review
identifies an execution error, the IA generates a corrective solution
and feeds it back to the EA to rectify the mistake before reattempting
the step.
3 EV ALUATION
A well-designed benchmark dataset should encompass tasks of
varying difficulty levels to thoroughly evaluate automated pen-
etration testing methods. Therefore, we selected VulHub as our
benchmark dataset. VulHub is an open-source collection contain-
ing numerous vulnerable Docker environments, allowing for quick
assessment of environmental attributes and exploitation difficulty
via relevant metrics, making it an ideal benchmark dataset for
penetration testing. We constructed a benchmark comprising 10
penetration testing targets to ensure a comprehensive evaluation.
The proposed method, acting as the attacker, was deployed on a vir-
tual machine running Ubuntu 22.04 LTS. It was provided with the
IP addresses of target hosts running on separate virtual machines
to conduct penetration tests. The attacker machine was equipped
with common tools and maintained network connectivity with the
victim machines via NAT, simulating real-world network condi-
tions while the attacker attempted to exploit vulnerabilities. We
used PentestAgent and Nessus as baseline methods for comparison.
PentestAgent is an LLM-based automated penetration testing sys-
tem that integrates a multi-agent design and Retrieval-Augmented
Generation technology. It enhances penetration testing knowledge
and automates various tasks, capable of operating with minimal
human intervention. Nessus is a widely used automated vulnera-
bility scanning tool with over 210,000 scanning plugins covering
approximately 85,000 vulnerabilities, enabling effective penetration
testing[3]. Due to space limitations, the detailed reports generated
by the methods cannot be fully presented here. We evaluated the
experimental results using stage-wise completion rate as the met-
ric, specifically examining each method’s ability to: successfully
scan target port services(Scan), complete vulnerability exploitation
attempts(Analysis), and identify the number of actually exploitable
vulnerabilities(Exploit). The experimental results are summarized
as Fig. 3.
Figure 3: Stage-wise Completion Rate.
The results demonstrate that the proposed method performs
effectively across all stages. PentestAgent often fails to collect tar-
get information due to anti-crawler mechanisms on websites and
underutilizes locally downloaded data. In contrast, our approach
equips the Vulnerability Analysis Agent with local technical docu-
mentation on bypassing anti-crawler measures, thereby improving
the success rate of data retrieval. While Nessus successfully con-
ducts scans and provides detailed reports, its output is primarily
intended for user awareness. For instance, Nessus frequently flags
issues such as outdated Ubuntu versions recommending upgrades or
ICMP timestamp responses potentially enabling attacks—findings
that are often impractical for actual penetration testing. In com-
parison, the proposed method achieves a higher success rate in
returning actionable and practically exploitable results.
We further analyzed the quantity of data collected by PentestA-
gent and AutoPen for each target host. The results, shown in Fig. 4,
indicate that AutoPen retrieves necessary information with superior
stability compared to PentestAgent. Although AutoPen downloads
a lower total volume of data due to stricter filtering criteria, it
more effectively completes the final penetration testing tasks. This
proves that our method reliably performs vulnerability information
retrieval and analysis, fully leverages the generative capabilities of
large language models, and achieves stronger vulnerability detec-
tion with relatively less external data.

to analyze the effectiveness of the Inspection Agent. Specifically,
we disabled the functionalities related to the IA and ran the pro-
cess manually without reasoning capabilities. The method without
the IA is denoted as AutoPe_noIA. The results indicate that while
AutoPen_noIA could successfully complete the environment recon-
naissance and vulnerability analysis tasks, it only managed to iden-
tify one supposedly exploitable vulnerability. Furthermore, upon
manual investigation, we found that the success report returned by
AutoPen_noIA claimed a successful DDoS attack—asserting that the
